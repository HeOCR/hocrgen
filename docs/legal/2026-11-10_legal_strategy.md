## **1\. Copyright in the Scans Themselves**

Whether a faithful photographic or flatbed scan of a two-dimensional public-domain printed book attracts a new copyright or neighbouring right depends on the jurisdiction, though the trend across all three regimes strongly favors the public domain.

### **United States Law**

Under the U.S. Copyright Act, 17 U.S.C. § 102(a), copyright subsists only in "original works of authorship." In *Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.*, 499 U.S. 340 (1991), the Supreme Court held that originality requires independent creation plus at least a minimal degree of creativity, explicitly rejecting the "sweat of the brow" doctrine (labor, technical skill, or financial investment).

* **Slavish 2D Reproductions (*Bridgeman*):** In *Bridgeman Art Library, Ltd. v. Corel Corp.*, 36 F. Supp. 2d 191 (S.D.N.Y. 1999), the court ruled that exact photographic reproductions of public-domain two-dimensional works of art are "slavish copies" lacking the modicum of creativity required for copyright, even when producing them requires high technical skill and specialized equipment.  
* **Subsequent Circuit Authority:** Later appellate decisions have consistently extended *Bridgeman*'s logic. In *Meshwerks, Inc. v. Toyota Motor Sales U.S.A., Inc.*, 528 F.3d 1258 (10th Cir. 2008\) (Gorsuch, J.), the Tenth Circuit held that highly detailed 3D digital wireframe models of vehicles lacked originality because the creators intended to faithfully replicate the existing physical object rather than introduce expressive variations. Similarly, in *ABS Entertainment, Inc. v. CBS Corp.*, 908 F.3d 405 (9th Cir. 2018), the Ninth Circuit held that digitally remastering pre-1972 sound recordings by optimizing technical parameters did not create a new copyrightable derivative work where the identity of the underlying work remained unaltered.  
* **U.S. Copyright Office Policy:** The *Compendium of U.S. Copyright Office Practices* (3d ed.) § 909.3 and § 313.4(A) explicitly states that a photograph or scan that is "merely a slavish copy" of a two-dimensional work—including digitization, de-skewing, color correction, or automated contrast/binarization adjustments—cannot be registered for copyright.

**U.S. Conclusion:** Faithful overhead or flatbed book scans of pre-1929 printed books attract **no copyright** under U.S. law.

### **Israeli Law**

Under the **Israeli Copyright Act, 2007** (*חוק זכות יוצרים, התשס"ח-2007*), Section 4(a)(1), copyright subsists in an "original" literary, artistic, dramatic, or musical work fixed in any form. Section 1 defines an "artistic work" to include "a photographic work" (*יצירת צילום*).

* **The Originality Requirement (*Interlego*):** Long before the 2007 Act, the Israeli Supreme Court in **CA 513/89 *Interlego A/S v. Exin-Lines Bros. S.A.***, PD 48(4) 133 (1994) (Chief Justice Shamgar) adopted the *Feist* standard into Israeli law, holding that investment of labor, technical skill, and capital ("sweat of the brow") is insufficient; a work must exhibit minimal creative expression. This principle was codified in the 2007 Act and reinforced in **CA 8485/08 *The FA Premier League Ltd. v. Israeli Sports Betting Council*** (2010) and **CA 7996/11 *Safecom Ltd. v. Raviv*** (2013).  
* **No Neighbouring Right for Simple Photographs:** Unlike Germany or Italy, Israeli copyright law has **no *sui generis* or neighbouring right** (*זכויות שכנות*) for non-original "simple photographs." A photograph either crosses the *Interlego* threshold of creative originality (framing, lighting, angle, artistic staging) or it receives zero copyright protection.  
* **Distinguishing Scholarly Reconstruction (*Qimron*):** In **CA 2790/93 *Eisenman v. Qimron***, PD 54(3) 817 (2000), the Israeli Supreme Court recognized copyright in Prof. Elisha Qimron's decipherment and reconstruction of the Dead Sea Scrolls fragment (4QMMT). Crucially, *Qimron* turned on the creative scholarly conjecture required to fill missing textual lacunae from puluhan fragments—not on mechanical photography of an intact page.  
* **Flagging Uncertainty in Israel:** While Israeli academic commentary (including Prof. Michael Birnhack and Tony Greenman) agrees that faithful 2D reprographic scanning lacks originality under *Interlego*, the Israeli Supreme Court has not issued a direct, published precedent squarely addressing automated library book digitization on all fours with *Bridgeman*. Even so, the risk that an Israeli court would find creative originality in Google ScanOps' uniform, industrial page-scanning process is exceedingly low.

### **European Union Law (DSM Directive Article 14 & National Related Rights)**

Under harmonized EU copyright law, a photograph qualifies for full copyright (70 years *post mortem auctoris*) only if it is the "author's own intellectual creation," reflecting free and creative choices (*Infopaq*, Case C-5/08; *Painer*, Case C-145/10). Automated book scans clearly fail this test.  
However, **Article 6 of the Term Directive (2006/116/EC)** permitted EU Member States to maintain national related/neighbouring rights for "other photographs" (non-original or "simple" photographs). Several Member States—most notably Germany, Italy, Austria, and Spain—maintained such rights.

* **Article 14 of the 2019 DSM Directive (EU 2019/790):** Following the German Federal Court of Justice's controversial *Museumsfotos* decision (**BGH, Dec. 20, 2018, I ZR 104/17**), which held that faithful reproductive photographs of public-domain paintings taken with a camera protected the museum under Germany's related right for simple photographs, the EU enacted Article 14 of the DSM Directive. Article 14 provides:*"Member States shall provide that, when the term of protection of a work of visual art has expired, any material resulting from an act of reproduction of that work is not subject to copyright or related rights, unless the material resulting from that act of reproduction is original in the sense that it is the author's own intellectual creation."*  
* **The Critical Statutory Gap ("Works of Visual Art" vs. Books):** By its express wording, Article 14 applies only to **"works of visual art"** (*Werke der bildenden Kunst* / *opere delle arti visive*). It does **not** explicitly cover **literary works** (such as printed text pages in historical books), although the European Copyright Society has argued that excluding literary works creates an illogical disparity. Because of this gap, you must look at how key Member States treat simple photographs of text pages:  
  * **Germany (§ 72 UrhG and § 68 UrhG):** Germany transposed Article 14 into **§ 68 UrhG**, stripping related-right protection under **§ 72 UrhG** (*Lichtbilder*, protected for 50 years from publication/creation) from faithful reproductions of public-domain "visual works" (*visuelle Werke*). For pure text pages, § 68 UrhG arguably does not apply (unless the page contains woodcuts, illuminated initials, or printers' ornaments). However, even under classic § 72 UrhG jurisprudence, German courts distinguish between a photograph (*Lichtbild*) and a **purely technical/reprographic copy** (*bloße Reproduktion*, such as a photocopy or automated flatbed/book-cradle scan), which enjoys no § 72 protection at all. Whether Google's overhead camera book-cradle scans of text pages could ever be claimed as § 72 *Lichtbilder* in Germany remains a minor theoretical gap in the statute, though Google has never asserted § 72 UrhG over public-domain books.  
  * **Italy (Art. 87–92 and Art. 32-quater LDA):** Italy transposed Article 14 into **Article 32-quater** of the Italian Copyright Act (Law 633/1941, *Legge sul diritto d'autore* or LDA). While Italy protects "simple photographs" (*fotografie semplici*) for 20 years from production under **Articles 87–92 LDA**, **Article 87(2) LDA expressly excludes** *"photographs of writings, documents, business papers, material objects, technical drawings and similar products."* Thus, faithful scans of printed book pages attract **no neighbouring right in Italy** under copyright law. *(Caveat on Italian Public Law:* Italy's Cultural Heritage Code, *Codice dei beni culturali*, D.Lgs. 42/2004, Arts. 107–108, allows Italian state libraries to restrict commercial reproduction of physical cultural heritage items from their collections. However, that is a domestic Italian administrative regulation, not a copyright or neighbouring right enforceable abroad).\*

## **2\. Contract or Terms-of-Use Restrictions**

Why do NLI and Google attach "non-commercial" language to public-domain scans? When Google launched the Google Books Library Project in 2004, its bilateral **Cooperative Digitization Agreements** with partner libraries (including the University of Michigan/HathiTrust, the British Library, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, and NLI) stipulated that while Google bore the cost of digitization and provided digital copies back to the partner library, the partner library had to restrict its hosted copies to **non-commercial use** and prevent bulk redistribution by third parties. That contractual obligation between Google and NLI explains why NLI labels Google-digitized books "public domain, non-commercial use" while labeling NLI's own in-house scans "public domain, free use."

### **Google's Front-Page "Usage Guidelines"**

The boilerplate front page inserted into Google Books PDFs states that the book is in the public domain and says: *"We also ask that you: \+ Make non-commercial use of the files... \+ Refrain from automated querying... \+ Maintain attribution."*

* **Not a Contract:** Under both U.S. common law and the Israeli Contracts (General Part) Law, 1973 (*חוק החוזים (חלק כללי), התשל"ג-1973*, §§ 2, 5), forming a contract requires a definite offer, acceptance, and *gemirut da'at* (manifested intent to create binding legal relations). Google's sheet uses precatory language ("We ask that you...", "guidelines") and describes itself as non-binding stewardship requests rather than conditional license terms.  
* **No Browse-Wrap Assent:** Even if framed as terms, embedding a text page inside a passive PDF file downloaded from a third-party repository provides neither prior conspicuous notice nor affirmative assent (*Nguyen v. Barnes & Noble Inc.*, 763 F.3d 1171 (9th Cir. 2014); *Specht v. Netscape Communications Corp.*, 306 F.3d 17 (2d Cir. 2002)).

### **(a) Routes B (Internet Archive) and C (Wikimedia Commons) — Binding on HeOCR?**

**No.** A fundamental distinction between property rights (like copyright) and contract rights is **privity of contract**:

* Copyright is a right *in rem* (good against the world). Because no copyright exists in the public-domain book or the slavish scan in the U.S. or Israel, neither Google nor a partner library has an *in rem* hook that travels with the digital file.  
* Contract is a right *in personam* (enforceable only against parties who assented to the contract). As Judge Easterbrook explained in the leading U.S. case on shrinkwrap contracts, *ProCD, Inc. v. Zeidenberg*, 86 F.3d 1447, 1454 (7th Cir. 1996): *"A copyright is a right against the world. Contracts, by contrast, generally affect only their parties; strangers may do as they please, so contracts do not create 'exclusive rights.' Someone who found a copy of SelectPhone on the street would not be affected by the shrinkwrap license."*  
* In Routes B and C, you download the files from Internet Archive (under IA's terms, which impose no restriction on public-domain items) or Wikimedia Commons (which explicitly offers PD files free of all restrictions). You never used Google's servers or NLI's API, and you never agreed to their terms. Neither Google nor NLI can bind you via contract in Routes B and C.

### **(b) Route A (NLI IIIF API) — Binding on HeOCR?**

**Potentially Yes (with unsettled defences).** When you register for an NLI IIIF API key and accept NLI's Terms of Use, you enter into direct contractual privity with NLI via a click-wrap or API agreement.

* **What NLI's Terms Say:** NLI's general website Terms of Use state that copyright in material belongs to NLI "unless explicitly stated otherwise" and prohibit copying, redistributing, or publishing "protected material" without prior written consent. Alongside the item-level metadata tag ("public domain, non-commercial use"), NLI's contractual position is that API access to Google-digitized scans is conditioned on non-commercial use.  
* **Is a Non-Commercial Contractual Condition on Public-Domain Material Enforceable?**  
  * **United States (Copyright Preemption under 17 U.S.C. § 301):** U.S. circuit courts are split. Under the Seventh Circuit's *ProCD* rule, a bilateral contract supplies an "extra element" (mutual assent) and is enforceable even when it restricts uncopyrightable public-domain data. Conversely, the Second Circuit in *Universal Instruments Corp. v. Micro Systems Engineering, Inc.*, 924 F.3d 32 (2d Cir. 2019\) and *MLGenius Holdings LLC v. Google LLC*, No. 22-610 (2d Cir. 2023\) held that § 301 preempts state breach-of-contract claims where the contract right asserted is merely the exclusive right to reproduce or adapt material and lacks a genuine extra element beyond a promise not to copy.  
  * **Israel (Unsettled — Public Policy & Standard Contracts Law):** Israel lacks an explicit statutory preemption clause equivalent to 17 U.S.C. § 301, and Israeli courts generally give broad weight to freedom of contract. However, three serious counter-arguments exist under Israeli law:  
    1. **Interpretation of NLI's Own Terms:** NLI's Terms of Use restrict copying and redistribution of *"the protected material"* (*החומר המוגן*) and explicitly begin most restrictions with *"Subject to the law of copyright..."* (*בכפוף לדיני זכויות יוצרים*). A strong textual argument exists that NLI's boilerplate prohibition applies only to copyrighted works, not works NLI itself labels "public domain." However, the specific catalog label "public domain, non-commercial use" would likely be read as a specific term overriding general phrasing.  
    2. **Standard Contracts Law, 1982 (*חוק החוזים האחידים, התשמ"ג-1982*):** Under Sections 3 and 4, an Israeli court can invalidate an "unduly disadvantageous condition" (*תנאי מקפח*) in a contract of adhesion.  
    3. **Public Policy (*תקנת הציבור*, Section 30 of the Contracts Law, 1973\) & The National Library Law, 2007:** Israeli legal scholarship (e.g., Prof. Michael Birnhack and Prof. Niva Elkin-Koren) argues that when a public statutory body—governed by the **National Library Law, 2007** (*חוק הספרייה הלאומית, התשס"ח-2007*), whose statutory mandate is making national cultural heritage accessible to the public—uses standard-form contracts to re-impose copyright-like monopolies on the public domain, such restrictions frustrate the statutory balance of the Copyright Act and offend public policy.  
    * **Why this remains risky/unsettled:** An Israeli court could distinguish NLI's restriction by noting that NLI is not greedily enclosing the public domain on its own initiative (since NLI releases its own scans as "free use"), but is merely honoring a mandatory flow-down clause in its contract with Google, which financed the scanning. Because this has never been litigated to judgment in Israel, treating Route A as contractually binding on you is the prudent assumption.

### **(c) Downstream Users of Your Released Datasets and Models**

**No—downstream users are not bound.** Even if you breached NLI's API contract in Route A by releasing derived crops or weights under CC0/CC-BY/Apache-2.0, a third-party company that downloads your dataset from Hugging Face or Zenodo has **zero contractual privity** with NLI or Google. Because neither NLI nor Google holds copyright in the scans (at least in the U.S. and Israel), NLI cannot sue a downstream commercial user for copyright infringement, nor can it enforce a contract against a third party who never agreed to NLI's Terms of Use. However, NLI could demand that *you* take down the dataset from Hugging Face/Zenodo, which would cast doubt on the permanence and commercial cleanliness of your release—violating your project's core rule.

## **3\. Derived Artefacts**

### **Line Crops and Glyph Crops**

* **Copyright Analysis:** Because neither the pre-1929 Hebrew text nor the faithful photographic scan is copyrighted in the U.S. or Israel, cropping a line or an individual Hebrew letter creates no copyright infringement. Furthermore, **typefaces and individual typographic glyphs** are not subject to copyright under U.S. law (37 C.F.R. § 202.1(a); *Eltra Corp. v. Ringer*, 579 F.2d 294 (4th Cir. 1978)), and any historical design right in a pre-1929 printed font has expired worldwide. Even in an EU jurisdiction like Germany where a theoretical § 72 UrhG *Lichtbild* argument might be raised over a full page scan, extracting isolated microscopic character glyphs and recombining them into randomized synthetic pages removes the visual integrity of the original photograph, making a neighbouring-right claim over a glyph bank implausible.  
* **Contractual Analysis (Route A only):** If you are bound by NLI's Route A API contract not to make commercial use of or redistribute the scans, publishing **line crops** under CC0/CC-BY for commercial use is still a recognizable redistribution of slices of the restricted image files. **Synthetic pages built from a glyph bank** are substantially more transformative and detached from any single scan, though a strict contractual clause barring commercial derivatives of API-fetched files could still leave a shadow of doubt.

### **Trained Model Weights (Tesseract tessdata\_best / Kraken)**

* **Copyright Analysis:** Trained neural network weights (LSTM/CNN parameters in Tesseract or Kraken) are abstract mathematical representations of statistical relationships between pixel features and Unicode codepoints. They do not contain copies of the historical book pages and cannot reproduce the training scans.  
  * **United States:** Under *Authors Guild v. Google, Inc.*, 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015), intermediate copying of books to extract non-expressive statistical information or enable search is transformative fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107\.  
  * **Israel:** On **December 18, 2022**, the **Israeli Ministry of Justice issued a formal legal opinion** (*חוות דעת משרד המשפטים: שימוש בתכנים מוגנים בזכויות יוצרים לצורך למידת מכונה*) concluding that ingesting even *fully copyrighted* works to train machine-learning models typically qualifies as Fair Use under Section 19 of the Israeli Copyright Act, 2007, and that the resulting model weights (absent memorization of expressive works) do not infringe copyright in the training corpus. Where the underlying works are **already in the public domain**, copyright risk in the weights is non-existent.  
  * **European Union:** Under **Articles 3 and 4 of the DSM Directive (2019/790)**, Text and Data Mining (TDM) is expressly permitted by statutory exception (Art. 3 for scientific research, against which contractual overrides are void under Art. 7(1); Art. 4 for any commercial or non-commercial purpose unless reserved via machine-readable opt-out).  
* **Contractual Analysis — Does Training & Releasing Apache-2.0 Weights Violate a "Non-Commercial Use" Clause?**  
  * **Routes B and C:** No contract applies, and Google's usage guidelines are non-binding requests. Releasing weights under Apache-2.0 is completely clean.  
  * **Route A (NLI):** As an open-source maintainer training models without charging money, your *act of training* is non-commercial. However, releasing the weights under Apache-2.0 explicitly authorizes downstream commercial exploitation. Do NLI's Terms of Use prohibit releasing statistical model weights trained on API images? NLI's Terms of Use prohibit copying, redistributing, or publishing "the protected material" itself; they do not contain modern AI-specific clauses claiming ownership over or restricting statistical model weights derived from viewing the images. Legally, model weights are not a copy or redistribution of the image file. Nevertheless, if NLI views commercial model release as circumventing its "non-commercial use" API tag, it could create institutional friction with NLI.

### **Realistic Exposure Breakdown**

* **Google:** **Negligible legal or reputational risk.** In 20+ years of the Google Books project, Google has never brought a copyright or contract claim against open-source projects, Wikimedia Commons, or the Internet Archive for reusing or stripping watermarks from public-domain scans. Google itself released tessdata\_best under Apache-2.0 and fought for transformative fair use in *Authors Guild v. Google*. The only thing to avoid is leaving the **"Digitized by Google" watermark/logo** on redistributed images in a way that could imply Google's trademark endorsement.  
* **National Library of Israel (NLI):** **Primary source of practical and reputational risk (Route A only).** Because you are based in Israel and use an authenticated NLI IIIF API key, NLI can easily identify bulk downloads. While NLI is highly unlikely to sue an open-source Hebrew OCR researcher for damages, NLI is contractually bound to Google to prevent commercial redistribution of Google-digitized volumes. If you publish full pages or large line-crop datasets from Route A under CC0/CC-BY, NLI could revoke your API key, send a formal takedown notice to Hugging Face/Zenodo, or object publicly within the small Israeli digital humanities community.  
* **Wikimedia Community & Internet Archive:** **Zero risk; positive reputational alignment.** Both communities explicitly reject restrictions on faithful scans of public-domain books and actively encourage open-source Hebrew OCR models and datasets.

## **5\. What Comparable Projects Do**

Open cultural heritage institutions and OCR/HTR projects have established clear precedents for handling Google-digitized public-domain scans:

* **Wikimedia Commons & Wikisource:** Commons policy (Commons:When to use the PD-scan tag and Template:PD-Art) explicitly follows *Bridgeman* and DSM Directive Article 14: mechanical or photographic scans of 2D public-domain works create no new copyright. Commons treats Google's front-page "usage guidelines" as non-binding requests that do not restrict the public domain. Volunteers routinely upload Google Books scans via tools like ia-upload or BUB (Bologna University Library upload bot, which created the bub\_gb\_ items on IA), remove the Google cover sheet and watermarks, and tag the files {{PD-old-100}} and {{PD-scan}} alongside {{Google Books}} for provenance. Hebrew Wikisource relies directly on these scans for proofread editions.  
* **Internet Archive (bub\_gb\_ / goog):** Between 2008 and 2016, the Internet Archive and community projects ingested hundreds of thousands of public-domain Google Books scans (e.g., identifiers ending in goog or starting with bub\_gb\_). IA assigns them Possible copyright status: NOT\_IN\_COPYRIGHT or the Creative Commons **Public Domain Mark 1.0**, strips Google's usage-guideline restrictions from the metadata, and provides unrestricted bulk access via IA's API.  
* **HathiTrust (pd-google):** Because HathiTrust's founding universities signed direct cooperative agreements with Google, HathiTrust distinguishes between pd ("Public Domain" — digitized by Internet Archive or member libraries, open for any use) and pd-google ("Public Domain, Google-digitized"). On pd-google volumes, HathiTrust stamps each page with Public Domain, Google-digitized / \[http\://www\.hathitrust.org/access\_use\#pd-google\](http\://www\.hathitrust.org/access\_use\#pd-google) and states that while the work is free of copyright restrictions, HathiTrust's contract with Google prohibits commercial reuse or automated bulk downloading from HathiTrust's servers. However, HathiTrust's Research Center (HTRC) distributes extracted features (token counts, bounding-box/page metrics) under open licenses.  
* **Europeana:** Under the **Europeana Public Domain Charter** and the **Europeana Publishing Framework**, Europeana requires data providers to submit faithful digitizations of public-domain works with the **Public Domain Mark (PDM)** or **CC0**, explicitly stating that digitization does not generate a new right. Where national libraries (such as Bayerische Staatsbibliothek or Austrian National Library) contribute Google-digitized volumes, many have transitioned to PDM after their initial commercial-exclusivity embargo periods with Google expired (typically 15 years after scanning in several European library contracts).  
* **Open OCR/HTR Ecosystems (Kraken, eScriptorium, HTR-United, CATMuS):** The flagship open HTR datasets in the Kraken/eScriptorium ecosystem—such as **CATMuS** (Coordinated Annotated Transcription of Multilingual Scripts, published on Hugging Face under CC-BY 4.0) and **HTR-United** catalogs—include line crops and page scans sourced from BnF Gallica, Internet Archive, and Wikimedia Commons. Notably, BnF Gallica's terms also assert that commercial reuse of Gallica's public-domain scans requires a commercial license fee; French academic and open-source HTR teams routinely release line-crop datasets and Kraken models under CC-BY 4.0 or Apache-2.0 regardless, relying on DSM Directive Art. 14 and the French *Loi pour une République numérique* (2016), while citing the source ARK/IIIF URL for provenance.

## **6\. Recommendation**

Given your strict project rule—*"no input may enter the training pipeline if its terms would later cast doubt on releasing the derived data or models openly, including for commercial use by third parties"*—the cleanest strategy combines **Options (ii) and (iii)** with a targeted carving for NLI:

> 1. **Exclude Route A (NLI's "public domain, non-commercial use" Google scans) from your open datasets and training pipeline**, while **freely using NLI's own "public domain, free use" scans.**  
   * *Why:* Even though NLI holds no copyright in the Google scans, your affirmative acceptance of NLI's IIIF API terms creates direct contractual privity in Israel. Using Route A scans to publish CC0/CC-BY line crops or Apache-2.0 models directly invites doubt over whether you breached NLI's API contract.  
   * *Actionable Exception:* Before permanently discarding NLI's Google-digitized books, **write to NLI's Digital Library / Ktiv team**. NLI actively supports Hebrew OCR and frequently grants written waivers for open-source research datasets, or may confirm that their 15-year embargo period under their original Google agreement has lapsed for specific batches. Unless and until you get a written waiver from NLI allowing CC0/CC-BY/Apache-2.0 release, keep Route A (non-commercial) out of the pipeline.  
> 2. **Accept Routes B (Internet Archive) and C (Wikimedia Commons / Wikisource) across the entire pipeline** (training, line crops, glyph banks, and benchmark page images).  
   * *Why:* In Routes B and C, there is **no copyright** in the underlying text or the slavish scan in the U.S. or Israel, and **no contractual privity** between you and Google or NLI. Google's front-page guidelines are non-binding requests that Wikimedia Commons and Internet Archive have disregarded for nearly two decades without a single legal challenge.  
   * *Deduplication Tip:* If a book appears on both NLI (Route A) and Internet Archive/Wikimedia Commons (Routes B/C), **ingest the file exclusively from Route B or C** and log the IA/Commons URL and SHA-256 hash as your source of record.

### **Defensive Implementation Steps for Routes B and C**

To make your use of Routes B and C maximally defensible (including against any theoretical EU national related-right claim or trademark issue), implement these four hygiene measures:

#### **1\. Strip Google Cover Pages and Watermarks**

Before extracting line crops, glyph banks, or benchmark pages, programmatically exclude page 1 (Google's boilerplate "Usage Guidelines" sheet) and crop out the bottom-corner "Digitized by Google" watermark. While copyright does not protect the scan, "Google" is a registered trademark; removing the logo prevents any claim of false endorsement or trademark dilution under the Lanham Act or Israeli trademark law, while you preserve factual provenance in text metadata.

#### **2\. Maintain Granular Per-Item Provenance Metadata**

Every line crop, glyph entry, and benchmark page should trace back via a deterministic ID (book\_id \+ page\_num \+ bbox) to a sources.jsonl or provenance.parquet manifest containing:

* book\_id, title, author, author\_death\_year (confirming greater than 70 years ago), publication\_year (confirming pre-1929).  
* acquisition\_route (internet\_archive, wikimedia\_commons, or nli\_free\_use).  
* source\_url (exact IA item URL or Commons file URL) and source\_sha256 of the ingested file.  
* digitizer\_provenance (e.g., "Digitized by Google; retrieved via Internet Archive").  
* transcription\_source\_url and license (e.g., Project Ben-Yehuda public-domain notice).

If any institution ever objects to a specific volume, this schema lets you filter out its line crops and glyphs from your Hugging Face / Zenodo dataset in minutes without invalidating the rest of the corpus.

#### **3\. Dual-Layer Licensing and NOTICE File Wording**

Because the underlying scans and texts are in the public domain, use **CC0 1.0** for raw page scans, line crops, and glyph banks, or **CC-BY 4.0** specifically limited to your *original selection, alignment, bounding-box annotation, and curation* of the dataset. Include the following statement in your README.md, Hugging Face Dataset Card, and NOTICE file:

Plaintext  
RIGHTS AND PROVENANCE NOTICE (HeOCR Project)

1\. Underlying Texts and Page Scans (Public Domain):  
   All historical book pages, line crops, and character glyphs in this dataset are derived from printed Hebrew books published prior to 1929 whose authors died more than 70 years ago. Under the laws of Israel (Copyright Act, 2007; CA 513/89 Interlego), the United States (17 U.S.C. § 102; Bridgeman Art Library v. Corel Corp., 36 F. Supp. 2d 191), and the European Union (Directive 2019/790, Art. 14), faithful photographic or mechanical reproductions of two-dimensional public-domain works do not generate a new copyright. HeOCR claims no copyright over the underlying page images or individual crops, which are marked with the Creative Commons Public Domain Dedication (CC0 1.0).

2\. Dataset Curation, Alignment, and Annotations:  
   To the extent that the selection, arrangement, line-level transcription alignment (paired with public-domain transcriptions from Project Ben-Yehuda), and bounding-box metadata constitute an original compilation, they are licensed under \[CC0 1.0 / CC-BY 4.0\].

3\. Source Attribution & Non-Endorsement:  
   Source scans were retrieved from public-domain collections hosted by the Internet Archive, Wikimedia Commons, and the National Library of Israel ("public domain, free use" collection). Where original digitization was performed by Google or partner libraries, provenance is recorded in \`provenance.jsonl\` for scholarly attribution. Neither Google, the National Library of Israel, Project Ben-Yehuda, nor Wikimedia endorses this project or its models.

4\. Notice and Takedown Procedure:  
   Every item in this dataset carries full provenance metadata. If you believe any specific item is subject to valid copyright or binding legal restriction, please open an issue at \[GitHub URL\] or contact \[Maintainer Email\] with the \`book\_id\` and legal basis, and contested items will be promptly reviewed and removed.

#### **4\. Documented Takedown Process**

Maintain a lightweight TAKEDOWN\_POLICY.md in the repository stating that upon receipt of a specific rights inquiry identifying a book\_id, the maintainer will immediately quarantine the affected volume from the public dataset download while investigating, and if removed, will log the removal in a public changelog.

### **Points Where a Qualified Israeli or U.S. Copyright Lawyer Should Be Consulted**

> 1. **Enforceability of NLI's IIIF API Terms under Israeli Law:** Whether NLI's item-level metadata tag ("public domain, non-commercial use") combined with its general Terms of Use creates an enforceable contractual bar in Israel against releasing derived crops or model weights, or whether such a restriction is void under Section 30 of the Israeli Contracts Law (public policy), the Standard Contracts Law of 1982, or NLI's statutory mandate under the National Library Law, 2007\.  
> 2. **Scope of NLI's Terms Regarding AI Model Weights:** If you wish to use Route A books *solely* to train Apache-2.0 model weights (without publishing the Route A crops or pages), an Israeli lawyer should review the exact Hebrew text of NLI's API agreement to confirm that statistical model weights fall outside the contractual definition of redistributing "the material."  
> 3. **Orphan Works, Rabbinic Commentaries, and Post-1928 Printers' Contributions:** For historical Hebrew books (such as *vilna shas* or 19th/early-20th-century editions containing multiple marginal commentaries, *haskamot* \[approbations\], or later editors' notes), verifying that *every* contributor on the cropped page died more than 70 years ago (for Israel/EU) and that the edition was published before 1929 (or without U.S. renewal/URAA restoration under 17 U.S.C. § 104A).  
> 4. **German § 72 UrhG Exposure for Full-Page Benchmarks Hosted on EU Mirrors:** If you plan to publish a benchmark of *full-page* scans and want total certainty for commercial users based in Germany, an EU/German IP specialist can confirm whether Google's overhead book-cradle scans of text-only pages fall outside § 72 UrhG as mere technical reproductions (*bloße Reproduktionen*) despite § 68 UrhG's textual limitation to "visual works."

Faithful scans of pre-1929 public-domain Hebrew books attract no copyright in the United States or Israel, and Google's front-page usage guidelines are non-binding requests that cannot restrict files you acquire through third-party repositories like the Internet Archive (Route B) or Wikimedia Commons (Route C). By contrast, downloading Google-digitized scans directly from the National Library of Israel's IIIF API (Route A) where NLI explicitly tags them "public domain, non-commercial use" places you in direct contractual privity with an Israeli institution bound by its own digitization contract with Google, creating enough contractual and reputational uncertainty to violate your project's strict commercial-cleanliness rule. Consequently, you should exclude Route A's "non-commercial" Google scans (unless NLI grants a written waiver), rely freely on NLI's "free use" scans alongside Routes B and C, strip Google's watermarks and boilerplate cover pages, and maintain item-level provenance metadata so your Apache-2.0 weights and CC0/CC-BY datasets remain unimpeachably open for any commercial or non-commercial use.
